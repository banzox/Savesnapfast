Saving a public post for later can be useful. Creators collect examples to study editing styles, social media managers keep references for a campaign, and educators often need material to discuss in class. The fact that a post is public, however, does not automatically make every use of it permissible.

This guide offers a simple workflow for deciding what to save, what to record with it, and when to ask the creator before doing anything more.

## Start with the purpose

Before copying a link or saving a file, write one sentence explaining why you need it. A clear purpose makes later decisions easier.

Common low-risk purposes include:

- keeping a reference for an editing or storytelling technique;
- reviewing your own post after publication;
- preparing a private presentation or classroom discussion where the use is permitted; and
- keeping a record of a creator's approval for a collaboration.

The purpose changes the next step. Saving a reference for private study is different from reposting it to market a product. If the intended use is public, commercial, or permanent, assume that you need additional permission unless you already have a licence that covers it.

## Make a short rights note

For each reference, keep a small note alongside the link. A spreadsheet, a notes application, or a project-management tool is enough. Include:

1. The creator or account name.
2. The original public URL.
3. The date you saved the reference.
4. Why you saved it.
5. Any permission, licence, or restrictions the creator stated.

This takes less than a minute and avoids a common problem: finding a file months later with no clue where it came from or whether it can be used. It also helps a team give credit correctly.

## Ask for permission in plain language

When your use is not clearly personal or private, ask the creator before publishing. Keep the message specific. Explain what you want to use, where it will appear, whether it will be edited, and whether it will be connected to a commercial project.

For example: “I would like to include a short excerpt of your public video in a paid workshop slide deck. The audience will be about 30 people, and I will credit your account and link to the original. Is that okay?”

Do not treat a missing reply as approval. Save a copy of a positive answer and follow any conditions the creator gives, including credit wording, time limits, or a request not to alter the work.

## Give useful credit

Credit should help people find the original work. When you have permission to share, include the creator's displayed name, their account or profile link where appropriate, and a link to the original post. Do not imply that the creator endorses you, your organisation, or your product unless they explicitly said so.

Attribution is respectful, but it is not a substitute for permission. A credit line does not automatically make an otherwise unauthorised commercial use acceptable.

## Keep private material private

Do not try to use tools or workarounds to access private, restricted, deleted, or follower-only material. A reference workflow should begin with content that is publicly available and should stop when access is limited by the original creator or platform.

## Review before publishing

Before a saved reference leaves your private workspace, ask these questions:

- Do I own this material or have the creator's permission?
- Does the intended use match the permission I received?
- Will the credit be clear and accurate?
- Does the use respect the platform rules and local law?
- Could a reasonable creator feel misled by the context in which I am using it?

If one answer is unclear, pause and ask. A permission-first workflow is not only safer; it builds better relationships with the people whose work inspires yours.

## A practical final rule

Save public references thoughtfully, keep their source details, and separate private inspiration from public reuse. When a project becomes public or commercial, permission and clear credit should be the default rather than an afterthought.
