Short videos move quickly, so small accessibility choices make a large difference. Captions, readable overlays, and understandable audio help viewers who are deaf or hard of hearing, people watching without sound, viewers using a small screen, and anyone who needs more time to process information.

Accessibility is not a final decorative step. It is part of planning a clear message.

## Write the main point before editing

State the one idea a viewer should remember after watching. If you cannot write it in a sentence, the video may be trying to do too much. A focused message makes captions, on-screen text, and visual examples easier to create.

## Add accurate captions

Auto-captions can be a helpful draft, but they need a review. Check names, technical terms, numbers, and punctuation. Fix timing when captions appear before or after the words. Captions should not cover a critical visual element or disappear before a reader has time to understand them.

When a sound is important to the story, describe it briefly. For example, use “[door closes]” or “[upbeat music]” when that information changes how the moment is understood.

## Keep on-screen text readable

Use a large, simple typeface and strong contrast between the text and its background. Avoid placing light text over a bright, detailed video. A solid background, soft shadow, or translucent panel can improve legibility without changing the style of the video.

Limit each line to a short phrase. Viewers should not have to pause a fast-moving clip just to read the explanation.

## Do not rely on colour alone

If a chart, instruction, or before-and-after example uses colour to communicate meaning, add a label, pattern, or spoken explanation. For instance, say “the completed step is the item marked with a check” rather than only “choose the green option.”

## Describe essential visual changes

When a visual action is the key information, make it understandable in words. A cooking video can name the ingredient being added. A screen-recording tutorial can say which button is selected. This benefits people who cannot see every detail and makes the video easier to follow at faster playback speeds.

## Check the audio mix

Background music should not bury the voice. Listen through a phone speaker as well as headphones, because the balance can change significantly. If a viewer needs the spoken instructions, lower the music during those lines.

## Test on a small screen

Before publishing, watch the video on a phone at normal brightness. Check that text does not sit beneath interface controls, captions do not overlap your own labels, and the key visual can be understood without pausing. This simple test often finds issues that are not obvious in an editing application.

## Final pre-publish checklist

- Is the central message clear in the first few seconds?
- Are captions accurate, timed well, and easy to read?
- Is text large enough and high contrast?
- Does the video still make sense with sound off?
- Are important visual changes explained in words?
- Can viewers understand choices without depending only on colour?

Clear, accessible short videos reach more people and communicate more respectfully. The goal is not perfection in one edit; it is a repeatable habit of checking whether the message is available to as many viewers as possible.
