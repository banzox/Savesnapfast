Good short videos usually begin before the camera is turned on. A short content brief keeps an idea focused and gives everyone involved the same definition of success. It does not need to be a large document; one page is often enough.

The goal is to make deliberate choices before editing makes them expensive to change.

## Define one audience and one outcome

Start with two questions:

1. Who is this video for?
2. What should that person understand, feel, or do after watching?

An audience can be specific without being invasive. “People learning basic phone photography” is more useful than “everyone.” An outcome can be modest: “understand how to clean a phone lens before taking a photo.” Avoid trying to teach a whole subject in one clip.

## Write the opening in plain language

The opening should set an honest expectation. It can be a question, a visual result, or a direct statement of the problem. Do not make a claim that the rest of the video cannot support.

For example, “Three ways to make a desk setup easier to film” is clearer than “The only setup trick you will ever need.” Honest openings create better retention because the viewer knows what they are about to receive.

## List the proof or examples you need

Before filming, list the shots, demonstrations, screenshots, or sources that make the message credible. If you are showing a process, identify each step. If you are quoting a creator or using a public reference, record the original URL and confirm whether permission is needed for your planned use.

A brief can include a simple shot list:

| Section | Visual | Spoken or on-screen message |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Opening | Finished result | “Here is the problem this solves.” |
| Step one | Close-up action | “Start by doing this.” |
| Step two | Screen or demonstration | “Then check this setting.” |
| Closing | Result and next step | “Try it with your own project.” |

## Plan for accessibility while writing

Note the caption style, any visual detail that needs a spoken description, and whether music could interfere with the voice. It is easier to film a clear demonstration than to repair an unclear one in post-production.

Also decide how long text will remain on screen. A helpful rule is to keep each caption or instruction visible long enough to read comfortably at normal playback speed.

## Decide what you will not include

Constraints are useful. A brief can state that the video will not make medical, legal, financial, or performance guarantees. It can also exclude private information, unverified claims, and material you do not have permission to use.

This protects the audience and makes the review process faster.

## Add a review checklist

Before publishing, compare the edit with the original brief:

- Does the opening match the actual lesson?
- Does every example support the main point?
- Are claims specific and supportable?
- Are captions and text readable?
- Do you have permission for any third-party material?
- Is the next step clear without being misleading?

If the answer to a question is no, revise the video or revise the brief. A content brief is a working tool, not paperwork. It helps you create short videos that are clearer, more respectful, and easier to improve over time.
